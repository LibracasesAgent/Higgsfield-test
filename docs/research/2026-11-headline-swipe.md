# Headlines and hooks from Julian's swipe file: what Libra keeps (2026-11-05)

Why: on the 4 Nov call Julian (media buyer) said the static designs are fine but the HEADLINES are weak. Feature names ("Hidden
pocket", "Gold side zips") say nothing; a headline should be catchy, a bit more aggressive, led by a benefit or a feeling (his example:
"Looks expensive. Wears three ways."). He also wants stronger video hooks for the first 3 s: hook rate 32% now, target 40-50%. He sent
a classic copywriting swipe file. This file takes what can be reused and fits it to our facts (PLAYBOOK §1, plus the §5 script lines
that spell them out) and our rules (CLAUDE.md rule 3, PLAYBOOK §9, `data/campaign.json`).
Tags: [V] verified in the source, [S] the source's claim (its test results, method not shown), [I] our inference.

Source: Gary C. Halbert, "Killer Advertising: A Do-It-Yourself Advertising Kit" ("349 Great Headlines, 152 Attention Grabber Words,
Sample Ads, Sample Letters", from his series "How To Promote Your Business For Less Than $1,000!"). It is a 41-page scan of 1980s
mail-order material: headlines on pp. 2-17, the word list on pp. 18-19, sample ads on pp. 20-26, letters on pp. 27-36 and a "Which
headline pulled best?" quiz on pp. 37-38 that cites John Caples. I read all of it from an OCR copy and checked the word list and the
quiz against the page images. The scan is copyrighted, so it is not committed, and this file quotes only one-line headline examples.

## What the source says

**The headline pages (pp. 2-17, ~3,400 words of headlines, no commentary)** [V]: in the OCR, "you" appears 104 times and "your" 59,
"how" 69 ("how to" 43), "new" 29, "amazing" 22, "secret" 17, "free" 6, the first-person "I" 19 and "my" 10. The subjects are mostly
making money, diets, health and sex, the categories Meta restricts today. The shapes that recur [I]:
- a specific personal benefit ("How To Get Out Of The Rat Race") or a curiosity gap ("The Amazing Secret Of A Marketing Genius Who Is
  Afraid To Fly"); the strongest headlines do both;
- the reader is "you", often named by situation ("To People Who Want To Write, But Can't Get Started");
- odd, exact numbers ("Pays Beginners Up To $16.50 An Hour", "Pierced By 301 Nails ... Retains Full Air Pressure");
- a twist after a dash or a full stop ("This Pen 'Burps' Before It Drinks, But Never Afterwards!");
- news words: new, now, announcing, at last, discovered, revealed.

**The quiz (pp. 37-38): 20 A/B pairs and the winners.** That the source says these is [V]; the lifts themselves are [S].
- A convenience beat an ingredient: "Push Button shave cream" pulled over 2 to 1 against "Moisturized shave cream" (no. 1).
- A specific personal benefit beat lofty words (no. 2). "You", curiosity and an implied remedy together won (no. 3).
- **Put the saving in the headline:** "Would you buy a $30.00 shirt on sale for $14.99?" beat "Half price sale on shirts!" and
  "worked every time ... for any product" (no. 4). "Would you buy a woman's $65 sweater for $19.99?" sold the sweaters out in two days:
  "curiosity and benefit in one headline" (no. 11).
- **Plain facts beat a pun:** "It's from Mexico. It's handmade. And it's only $30.00" beat a pun on the jacket's name and sold 50
  jackets in 3 days (no. 5; the key prints this explanation under no. 12).
- Naming the audience pulled 20% more ("Car owners! Save one gallon ..."). Adding a benefit to a headline that names its audience
  pulled 27% more ("Dry up hay fever!" over "Hay fever!") (nos. 6, 7).
- "This is the ad we had to cancel three months ago because we sold them so fast" pulled 3 times as well. It was first run for women's
  handbags (no. 8).
- A headline below the picture got about 20% more response, "because a picture is a better eye-catcher than words" (no. 9).
- A true story told as it happened sold out on the first day ("We first saw these in the Crillon Hotel ... in Paris", no. 10).
- A message repeated too often bored the audience; the fresh line pulled twice as well (no. 13).
- Four words that offered free information pulled 50% better (no. 16). Practical advice to a beginner won (no. 17).
- The winner came from asking buyers WHY they bought: "the customer literally wrote the ad" (no. 18).
- "Free" and "how to" worked their "customary magic": of five horse-ad headlines, "Free lessons ..." ranked first (no. 19).
- A superlative label added nothing: "Now try the vitamin of champions" tied with "Now try this vitamin" (no. 20).
- [V] The answer key is partly scrambled: the explanations of nos. 5 and 12 are swapped, and no. 14's explanation describes the
  losing line. Only the pairs whose explanation matches the question are used here.

**The 152 attention grabber words (pp. 18-19)** [V]: three columns of nouns and phrases, most of them from business and investment
newsletters ("Views From An Investment Pro", "Investor's Roundtable", "Tax-resistant", "Nest-Egg") [I: section titles for a
newsletter]. Few of them fit a handbag ad. The ones that do are grouped by job below.

**The sample ads and letters (pp. 20-36)** [V], and what each shows about structure:
- A face cream "used by Hollywood stars": a curiosity headline, a question to the reader, the story of the discovery, how easy it is
  to use, a wall of short quotes with names, a no-risk refund, a coupon, a P.S.
- A Halley's Comet medallion: a news hook, and every subhead is a reason or a push (pure silver, limited series, once every 76
  years, price held for 30 days, one per customer, a bonus, "speed is important").
- Gold coins, "Who else wants to make a bundle?": holds back the answer ("I'll tell you in a moment"), compares it with the
  alternatives in a table, gives reasons with bold lead-ins, and offers a free report "with no obligation".
- A blackjack "secret" and "a marketing genius who is afraid to fly": a paradox or a flaw as the hook, then why it works and specific
  results. The second ends with six conditions that say who it is NOT for.
- "How to collect money from Social Security at any age!": a how-to headline over a long list of "How to ..." bullets.
- A long personal classified ad in the first person, with adult themes. It has a strong voice and a "7 things I do NOT want" list;
  nothing else in it fits a brand.
- The letters: a Rolls-Royce dealer (a photo, three reasons the price is low, status, a P.S. and a "mystery reason" kept for the
  phone call); a coat-of-arms report (the reader's own surname, a discovery story, an honest line about what it is not, a gift idea);
  a copper penny glued to the letter to earn the read, with both reasons for it given at once; and the Byrd polar expedition
  invitation (the price and "some discomfort" in the second paragraph, then the privilege, vivid detail and a real date).
- The order they share [I]: a hook (a benefit or an open question) → why it works → proof → the offer → an easy next step → a P.S.
  That is already the order of our videos. A static carries the hook, the picture, one proof and the offer.

### Five rules for a static headline

1. **Say the payoff, not the part** (Julian; quiz no. 2). If a line could be a label on a spec sheet ("Gold side zips"), rewrite it as
   what happens for her: "Keys get a spot. So does your phone." Ask "so what?" once and write the answer.
2. **3-8 words in two or three short beats, the strongest last** ("Looks expensive. Wears three ways."). The plain-facts jacket line
   beat the pun (quiz no. 5): no wordplay that has to be decoded ("ZIP IT. GO.", "Keys? Found." were B036's).
3. **Name the moment, then pay it off.** Naming the situation and adding the benefit beats either alone (quiz nos. 6-7: +20%, +27%):
   "Busy platform? Passport in the pocket facing you." The picture shows the answer, not the problem (PLAYBOOK 4n), and the words sit
   under or beside the picture (quiz no. 9).
4. **The offer is the loudest element; the headline is the second voice.** Show the saving as a % or "half price" (quiz nos. 4, 11),
   never a price figure in an AI ad. Say "free" only of what is free (the Hobo Bag's matching pouch wallet, free US shipping). No
   superlative labels (quiz no. 20; compliance blocks the ranking ones).
5. **Aggressive means confident and specific, not false.** Use a challenge ("Can you spot the hidden pocket?"), a command ("Don't put
   the coffee down."), a contrast ("It's anti-theft. It just doesn't look it."). Never an absolute (theft-proof, "nobody can"), fake
   urgency or scarcity, a callout of who she is, or an invented quote. When a customer said it, use her words verbatim with her name
   (quiz no. 18).

### Five rules for a video hook (the first 3 s)

1. **One sentence, at most 10 words** (14 is `ideas.py`'s hard cap). The first word lands at 0.0 s, the line is on screen in the first
   frame and the bag is in that frame (the scorecard wants the first word by 0.35 s).
2. **Open a question the picture answers within 3 s:** a test ("Can you spot the hidden pocket on this bag?"), a mistake ("Do you make
   this mistake on a packed train?"), a reason why ("Here's why this pocket is on the back, not the front."). By about 2.5 s the hand
   slides the passport in.
3. **The first shot shows the line happening:** a hand at the back pocket, the clasp snapping with its own click, the strap swap,
   never the bag just sitting there (`ideas.check`: the first `show` tag is a movement or a person; quiz no. 9). The library has few
   street or train clips (PLAYBOOK §12), so crowd hooks open on the hand at the pocket or the bag worn, and the crowd lives in the
   words.
4. **Specific beats clever, and both beat a superlative.** Drop "the most ... you'll see today". Use concrete nouns (passport,
   coffee, platform) and true small numbers (one hand, three ways, two straps).
5. **Name the situation in her words, never the person:** "Heading somewhere crowded this weekend?", not "Nervous traveller?" (Meta's
   personal-attributes policy). The four hooks are four formulas on four different first pictures, with the offer in at most one;
   don't open on "Black Friday prices are live" again. The tracker's Insights column holds each version's hook rate (3-s plays /
   impressions): keep the formula that takes a project past 40% and test a new one in place of the weakest.

### Where the source and our rules disagree

1. **First-person testimonial headlines** ("They Laughed When I Sat Down At The Piano", "How I Made A Fortune With A 'Fool Idea'"):
   our voice is "you" and never an invented "I" (compliance TESTIMONIAL). Only a named, approved review, verbatim.
2. **Celebrities, doctors and experts as proof** ("Used By Hollywood Stars", "Doctors Prove 2 Out Of 3 Women ..."): no celebrity use
   (compliance), no doctors or health (PLAYBOOK §9).
3. **Health, diet, beauty and "miracle" claims** (most of the source): never.
4. **Scarcity and deadlines:** "we sold them so fast" (quiz no. 8, first used for handbags), "very limited series", "one per customer",
   "price guaranteed only for 30 days", "only five left". We have no stock data (compliance blocks sold out / limited stock), and
   campaign.json allows only the real dates ("ends tonight" only on 30 Nov, "last chance" from 20 Nov).
5. **Risk reversal** ("100% satisfied or your money back, no questions asked"): the Black Friday terms make sale items final sale
   until 30 Nov, and compliance blocks money-back, easy returns and risk-free while the sale runs. The Hobo Bag's 1-year warranty is
   the trust line we can say.
6. **Price figures in the headline** (quiz nos. 4, 11): fine on our real `value` static (the site's price); never in an AI ad (the
   model draws wrong numbers) or in ad copy (prices change). Use the % or "half price".
7. **Callouts by personal attribute** ("For The Woman Who Is Older Than She Looks", "An Open Letter To Every Overweight Person In
   Portland", "Do You Have These Symptoms Of Nervous Exhaustion?"): Meta's personal-attributes policy. Name the situation ("Busy
   platform?"), never the person.
8. **Fear, death and shock** ("This Is Marie Antoinette, Riding To Her Death", "Is The Life Of A Child Worth $1 To You?", the
   bank-panic penny letter): violence and death words are blocked, and anti-theft ads stay calm (2026-11-all-time-best-ad.md §3: no
   snatch, no crime statistic).
9. **Absolutes and guarantees** ("Guaranteed To Go Thru Ice, Mud Or Snow, Or We Pay The Tow!", "... Has Never Yet Failed"): never
   theft-proof (compliance refuses the word even in a denial: "No bag is theft-proof." fails), and no guarantee we can't back.
10. **Adult headlines and the personal classified:** out (Meta's adult-content rules).
11. **Empty superlatives** ("amazing" 22 times, "the greatest", "the ultimate"): the source's own quiz says they add nothing (no. 20).

### How to use it

- In `ideas.json`, a static's `headline`, an `ad`'s first story line or a `story` static's `hook` takes one formula above (3-8
  words; a `story` hook 3-10) while the % stays the hero element. For videos, H1-H4 are four different formulas on four different
  first pictures (`ideas.check` asks for 3+ hook types and four different openings).
- Check a new line before it goes into a sheet: `python3 pipeline/compliance.py --text "<line>" --product "<bag>" [--theme "black friday"]`.
- Rotate: each line once a batch, never an earlier static's line (the check refuses repeats). "Looks expensive" is already in 24
  earlier lines (scripts, hooks, captions): pair it with a new second beat, or rest it.
